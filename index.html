The hook · Honors thesis

Sculpture in Motion: No Gears, No Linkages

A kinetic sculpture built without gears, or rigid linkages, whose shape memory polymer units generate silent and slow motion. I designed and built it end to end: concept, material choice, laser-cut parts, soldered circuits and a screwed-together final assembly.

01

The concept

Material as mechanism.A 3D-printed shape-memory polymer is 3D printed into a flat, temporary shape, then heated to curl back, creating subtle and silent movement without gears.
Electrically integrated system temperature control system.Nichrome wire embedded during printing turns every panel into its own electrically driven actuator.
The story.The landscape joins Nepal’s terrain and New York City’s, linked by the Koshi and Hudson rivers; the blue panels representing the river move when heated.

The context

RoleDesigner, researcher & builder · Advisor: Prof. Utter
TeamIndividual honors thesis
Timeline30 weeks
ToolsFusion 360 · Bambu Studio · Laser cutter · Soldering iron · Arduino

Problem & constraints

  • No conventional mechanisms: every bit of motion had to come from the material itself from its shape memory property.
  • Even heating: uneven temperature makes a panel bend only partway, or unpredictably.
  • Built to repeat: many identical moving panels, so every step had to give the same result each time.

Process map — how I built it

  1. 01ConceptTerrains, river link, panel layout
  2. 02MaterialPLA + TPU shape-memory panels
  3. 03CADFusion 360 models + wire fixture
  4. 04SimulateWire count & spacing vs. ΔT
  5. 05Print + embedNichrome laid in mid-print
  6. 06Laser-cutBase and panels to shape
  7. 07Solder + screwWire circuits; screwed joints
  8. 08Control & testArduino + temperature sensor

Photos & CAD

Highlights

  • Simulation before fabrication: the best wire layout was found before printing, so fewer test prints were wasted.
  • Fixture-driven repeatability: the fixture places the wire the same way in every panel.
  • Modular, serviceable build: screwed, not glued! joints let any panel be swapped without a rebuild.
  • Production flow: print → embed wire → laser-cut → solder → assemble, repeated for each panel.
The results

10KConstrained in max ΔT at optimal spacing

A working kinetic sculpture that moves without gears or linkages, built from repeatable shape-memory panels with embedded heaters.

The hook · Senior capstone

B.R.A.C.E. — Adaptive Knee Brace

A carbon-fiber 3D-printed brace that mechanically locks out hyperextension and auto-adjusts compression from pressure-sensor feedback.

02

The context

RoleTeam facilitator · electromechanical integration · design and prototyping
TeamTeam B.R.A.C.E. — 12 people
Timeline30 weeks
Tools3D printing (carbon fiber) · pressure sensors · [CAD tool]

Problem & constraints

  • Comfort first: athletes must wear it through full practices and games.
  • Biomechanics: stop hyperextension without restricting normal knee range of motion, or hurting major arteries around knee · provides automated compression based on changing muscle expansion
  • Sports regulations: the design must stay legal for competitive play.

Process map — human-centered design flow

  1. 01InterviewCollege athletes as stakeholders
  2. 02RequirementsBiomechanics + regulations
  3. 03Proof of conceptParallel concept builds
  4. 04Down-selectScore vs. requirements
  5. 05IntegrateLock + sensor dial + shell
  6. 06Test & presentPoster presentation

Photos & CAD

Highlights

  • Requirement — prevent hyperextension: medial and lateral hinges with a hard stop at the hyperextension limit, while normal bending stays free.
  • Requirement — adaptive compression: pressure sensors drive a dial that tightens or loosens compression along the wire path as the athlete moves.
  • Requirement — comfort through full play: an open patella window lets the kneecap track naturally, and the carbon-fiber printed shell stays light.
  • Requirement — quick to fit: a 4-piece 3D-printed shell goes on around the knee, and single parts swap out between iterations.
The results

Turned athlete interviews into a requirements-driven, integrated prototype combining a mechanical hyperextension stop with sensor-automated compression.

High-Performance Electric Dragster

Modeled an optimal 4.7 s race time, then traded it against braking, cost and manufacturability to ship a 2:1 AWD build with a servo brake triggered at ~385 in.

03

The context

RoleChassis & wheels lead · manufacturing planner
Team4 engineers
Timeline[One semester]
ToolsCNC mill · lathe · waterjet · 3D printing · laser cutter

Problem & constraints

  • Competing goals: acceleration vs. braking vs. controllability vs. manufacturability.
  • Cost penalties: each extra battery added cost that offset its speed gain.
  • Stopping distance: braking had to engage reliably at a fixed track position.

Process map — manufacturing routing

  1. CNCAluminum brake stopTight-tolerance fit
  2. LATHEAluminum axlesRoundness + custom geometry
  3. JETWaterjet chassisPrecision profile
  4. TPUPrinted wheelsTraction
  5. PLAHubs, servo mount, brake partsFast iteration
  6. LASERPlywood battery mountLight, quick to cut

Photos & CAD

Highlights

  • All-wheel drive: the motor gear drives the front axle and a belt and pulleys carry power to the rear, so all four wheels push.
  • 2:1 fixed gearing: close to the 2.1 modeled optimum, balancing launch acceleration against top speed.
  • Programmed braking: an Arduino-timed servo drives a linear stop into the axle stop at ~385 in.
  • One battery, by design: a second battery gained ~2 s but lost it to the penalty and cost.
  • Grip and precision: treaded TPU wheels, a CNC-milled brake stop, lathe-turned axles and a waterjet-cut chassis.
The results
4.7 sModeled optimal race time
2:1Gear ratio (2.1 modeled optimum)
~385 inCoded brake engagement point
6Manufacturing processes coordinated

Analysis-driven decisions on gear ratio, battery count and drivetrain, plus a six-process manufacturing plan, delivered a working AWD dragster on schedule.
